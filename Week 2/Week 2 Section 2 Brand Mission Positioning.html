Section 2

Brand: Mission and Positioning

Clarify the company’s purpose, market position, unique value, and identity.

Key Takeaways

  • A mission statement gives marketing strategy a clear purpose and direction.
  • Positioning explains why the offering is the best fit for a defined audience, while a USP turns that distinction into a compelling sales message.
  • Brand identity reflects what the organization intentionally communicates; brand image reflects what customers actually believe.

Before we can explain to people who we are and what we offer, we have to be crystal clear on what that is. The clearer the message, the easier it will be to communicate it to your customer. If you do not know what you offer, why should your customer understand its value?

We are going to put several items under this category, all relating to the brand, company, or product.

Mission Statement

Let’s start at the core of everything. When you are looking at a company to develop a strategy for, make sure you know what the mission is. What is our purpose? What do we want to offer the world? Most well-established companies already have some sort of mission statement or internal brand identity that guides them. This will be at the center of the content you create and the message you send.

You can see from the mission statements that they become, or should become, the guiding principles behind all strategy in the company.

Positioning and Unique Selling Proposition

Some people interchange positioning and unique selling proposition (USP). Others differentiate them by saying that USP refers to specific product attributes, whereas positioning describes a broader, company-wide position in relation to competitors. Here is the framework we will use:

Excerpt from Modern Marketing Principles
Positioning: the strategic process of defining and portraying brands or products in ways that cause the ideal customers to perceive them as the best solution for their needs.
Positioning statement: a succinct expression of a product’s market position.

The positioning statement serves as the foundation for a wide range of marketing activities. It is developed at the end of the positioning process, after a product’s market position is determined. Positioning statements are not advertising copy or product slogans, but the statement should influence the creation of those and other marketing elements.

For (target audience), product/service is (concise description). It is ideal for (best use or application) because (primary benefit or differentiation).

Unique Selling Proposition (USP): an adaptation of the product positioning statement for use in sales dialogues and communications.

The USP conveys the most compelling benefit of the product in terms a customer should easily understand. For example, Warby Parker is a direct-to-consumer eyeglasses retailer whose current USP is: “Pick 5 frames to try on at home (for free).”

1. First, look at the company’s positioning

  • What sets you apart from your competitors?
  • What unique or salient features does your product have?
  • What problem do you solve for your consumers?
  • What makes your product special?

2. Now, create your positioning statement

3. Next, craft your unique selling proposition

What is a succinct Unique Selling Proposition to use to sell the product to consumers? What clever USP can you create from your positioning knowledge?

Brand Identity and Brand Image

At this point, you have established your mission and laid out your unique selling proposition. Next, it is time to discuss branding, namely brand identity and brand image. The easiest way to differentiate the two might be this: While brand identity is more about how the company sees itself, brand image is how others view the company.

Brand identity includes “the intentional and strategic elements that a company uses to present itself to its audience.” Brand identity “expresses what a brand stands for through distinct, recognizable elements that set it apart from competitors.” — Harvard Business Review

Brand identity is internal and proactive. It is what you say about yourself and how you express it.

A company proactively creates a brand identity and then communicates that to the public. Creating this identity includes putting together visual elements to help customers easily identify the brand and make accurate associations with it.

A brand identity guide could include

  • Logo
  • Color palette
  • Typography (fonts) and visual style
  • Brand words or phrases
  • Brand personality or emotional element

If brand identity is internal and proactive, brand image is the external and reactive counterpart. It is what people actually think or believe about you.

Sometimes there is a gap between brand identity and brand image, and the public does not have the same image the company desires. This can happen because the company does not communicate the brand clearly. It can also happen when a customer’s experience with the brand does not match what the company is saying. Brand image also includes brand associationsBrand associations are the ideas, qualities, feelings, images, and experiences that people mentally connect with a brand., which develop through every significant experience a person has with the product.

Have you ever purchased a product and felt extremely disappointed when it did not live up to the hype? That is a gap in brand image: the image in your mind does not match what the company represented.

This brings us back to the concept of clarity. As marketers developing a marketing strategy, we can clearly establish what we want the brand to be known for and communicate it consistently.

Workshop Brand Identity

Write down three to five vivid words or phrases that you want to come to mind when people hear your brand name. These are the brand associations you want customers to recall immediately when they see your product name or logo.

  • Descriptive words
  • Product attributes or features: What salient features do customers care about? What sets the product apart? What value does it bring?

What brand personality or voice do you want the brand to have? Examples include formal or informal, casual, approachable, affluent, or elite.

Now look at those associations and the personality you described. What visual elements represent them and align with your target market’s preferences?

  • Color palette
  • Fonts
  • Logo, if you do not have one
  • Special icons or artwork

Your brand guide will include these visual elements, along with the brand personality or voice and the verbal phrases or slogans you want to use.
