Section 2

Short-Term Memory and Related Strategies

Use the brain’s predictable shortcuts to reduce friction and guide attention.

Key Takeaways

  • Chunk related content and avoid a wall of text when visitors first arrive.
  • Simplify choices, highlight a recommended path, and make important actions distinctive.
  • Use familiar website patterns so people can rely on existing mental models.

We often do not have the capacity—or motivation—to read every word and fully weigh every option. Good digital design helps people find what matters, make decisions, and move forward without unnecessary friction.

The following principles are commonly known as the “Laws of UX.” Their design implications are adapted from Laws of UX.

Miller’s Law: Reduce Cognitive Load

Avoid the dreaded “wall of text,” especially above the fold when someone first arrives. Organize related content into chunks using headings, scale, dividers, color, and space.

Use judgment: long-form prose may be appropriate for an origin story or article, but high-priority landing-page content should be easy to scan.

Hick’s Law: Simplify Decisions

The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices. More choices can create overload and stress.

Action items
  1. Identify the most important action.
  2. Remove unnecessary complexity without removing useful information.
  3. Highlight the recommended option.
  4. Break complex tasks into smaller steps.

Von Restorff Effect: Make What Matters Distinctive

The Von Restorff effect, also called the Isolation Effect, predicts that when multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs is most likely to be remembered.

Action items
  • Make important information or key actions visually distinctive.
  • Use restraint so emphasized elements do not compete or look like ads.
  • Do not rely on color alone; include shape, label, weight, or placement.
  • Use motion carefully for people with motion sensitivity.

Jakob’s Law: Use Familiar Mental Models

People spend most of their time on other sites. They prefer your site to work like sites they already know rather than relearning basic interactions.

Mental Model

A mental model is what a user believes about how a system works. It helps the user predict what will happen and shapes how they interact with an interface.

Action items
  • Learn your audience’s expectations and remember that they change over time.
  • Use familiar elements and interaction patterns.
  • Use consistent page templates throughout the site.
