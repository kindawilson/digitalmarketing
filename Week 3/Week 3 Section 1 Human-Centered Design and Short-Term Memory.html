Key Takeaways

  • Humans can only process so much information at a time. The human brain takes shortcuts. Much of our design is based on these concepts.

UX and UI

User eXperience (UX) is the complete experience a person has while using a product, service, or digital space. On a website, UX includes whether visitors can find what they need, understand what to do next, complete a task without frustration, and feel confident during the process.

A good user experience meets the user's needs, is easy to understand and learn, and creates a positive emotional response. In other words, UX is not one button or one page; it is the full journey from the moment someone arrives until the moment they leave or complete a goal.

User Interface (UI) is the part of that experience people can see and interact with. It includes elements such as colors, typefaces, menus, buttons, images, spacing, forms, and page layouts. UI and UX are closely connected, but they are not the same.

UX and UI matter in digital marketing because every ad, email, landing page, and website asks the audience to pay attention and take some kind of action. Marketing may create interest, but the experience determines whether that interest turns into a click, sign-up, purchase, or return visit.

We’re going to be covering some things that help that user experience (including the user interface). Things that we’ll cover include clear navigation, readable text, useful information, and an obvious call to action. When those pieces work together, people are more likely to trust the brand and continue the relationship.

Human-Centered Design

One framework that will guide our creations and help us improve the user experience is called human-centered design (HCD). So many times as marketers - or business people - we have a tendency to design things around the way that we think people should be. But in the digital world, it’s important to design around real human beings and the way they actually behave - flaws and all. This is the core of human-centered design.

Excerpt from The Design of Everyday Things

Human-Centered Design puts the human needs, capabilities, and behaviors first, and then designs in ways to accommodate those needs, capabilities, and behaviors.

Human-centered design starts with an understanding of humans and human behavior. This partly has to be learned from observation, because humans aren’t always aware of their reasoning or motivation behind doing things. They may not even be aware that they’re operating in a certain way.

Human-centered design begins with real people's needs, abilities, behaviors, and limitations. Instead of expecting users to adapt to a design, the designer studies how people actually think and act, then creates and tests material that fits those patterns. This process can include interviews, surveys, observation, prototypes, and usability testing. It also requires designers to consider accessibility and recognize that users may be distracted, rushed, unfamiliar with the topic, or using a phone rather than a laptop.

Short-Term Memory and Shortcuts

One very important factor that will influence everything that we create is how we as humans have limited short-term memory.

Short-term or working memory (STM) retains the most recent experiences or material that is currently being thought about. It is the memory of the “just present”. Information is retained automatically and retrieved without effort, but the amount of information that can be retained this way is severely limited.

As humans, we’re limited to how much information we can process or remember at one time. The limit of short-term memory is approximately 7 (+/ - 2) items at a time, with that number going to 10 to 12 if the items are constantly being repeated in your head. (Or you’re using special techniques like mnemonics to remember it.)

Another important concept here is cognitive load, or the amount of mental resources or mental effort being used in your working memory at any time. Any time we have more on our mind, are tired, are juggling too much mentally - that takes up more mental space.

Because of this short-term memory and cognitive load, the human brain ends up taking shortcuts. This will help us in our digital design. (In fact, an entire field called Gestalt Design is based on Gestalt psychology and related to these shortcuts.) We can learn about those shortcuts and learn how to design very effective digital marketing assets.
