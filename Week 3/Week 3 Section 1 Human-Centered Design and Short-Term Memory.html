UX and UI

User eXperience (UX) is the complete experience a person has while using a product, service, or digital space. On a website, UX includes whether visitors can find what they need, understand what to do next, complete a task without frustration, and feel confident during the process.

A good user experience meets the user’s needs, is easy to understand and learn, and creates a positive emotional response. UX is not one button or one page; it is the full journey from arrival through completion of a goal.

User Interface (UI) is the part of that experience people can see and interact with: colors, typefaces, menus, buttons, images, spacing, forms, and page layouts. UI and UX are closely connected, but they are not the same.

UX and UI matter in digital marketing because every ad, email, landing page, and website asks the audience to pay attention and act. Marketing may create interest, but the experience determines whether that interest becomes a click, sign-up, purchase, or return visit.

Human-Centered Design

Human-centered design (HCD) asks us to design around real human beings and the way they actually behave—flaws, distractions, habits, and all—not around the way we think people should behave.

Excerpt from The Design of Everyday Things

Human-centered design puts human needs, capabilities, and behaviors first, and then designs in ways that accommodate them.

It begins with an understanding of people and their behavior. Observation matters because people are not always aware of the reasons behind their actions or even that they are acting in a recurring pattern.

Instead of expecting users to adapt to a design, the designer studies how people think and act, then creates and tests material that fits those patterns. Interviews, surveys, observation, prototypes, usability testing, and accessibility checks all support this process. The designer also accounts for people who may be rushed, distracted, unfamiliar with the topic, or using a phone.

Short-Term Memory and Cognitive Load

Short-term, or working, memory holds the recent material a person is actively thinking about. It is automatic and easy to retrieve, but severely limited.

People can process only so much at one time. The familiar estimate is about seven items, plus or minus two, although context and repetition affect capacity.

Cognitive load is the amount of mental effort working memory is using at a given moment. Being tired, distracted, or already juggling information leaves less capacity for an interface. Because of these limits, the brain takes shortcuts. Understanding those shortcuts helps us design clearer, more effective digital marketing.
