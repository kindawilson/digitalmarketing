Section 6

Usability Testing

Key Takeaways

  • Use A/B testing to compare responses to simple design alternatives.
  • Use usability testing to observe people completing specific tasks in a more complex experience.
  • Ask participants to think aloud, observe without coaching, and follow up with neutral questions.
  • Test the design—not the participant—and treat problems and negative feedback as opportunities to improve.

Choosing a Testing Method

When we create any type of design, we always want to test them and see how our user responds. For a simple, single design such as a product landing page or an ad design. We could do that through A/B testing, either with a survey or by running the ad live in a split test to see the response rate for each.

For a more complex design, such as the user journey from the homepage through the purchase process, we can do usability testing. Usability testing is task oriented and tests specific things that we want the user to be able to do, such as look up product information and find an item and add to cart.

Usability Testing Overview

Content below includes excerpts from and is adapted from: Contextual Inquiry vs. Usability Testing

Usability testing evaluates the usability of a product or interface in a controlled setting, often with specific tasks

Core Objectives

Usability Testing Objectives:

  • Identifying flaws: Focuses on uncovering issues in the user interface or experience;
  • Enhancement of user interaction: Aims to make the product more intuitive and user-friendly;
  • Performance measurement: Evaluates efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction.

Methodological Approaches

Usability Testing: Conducted in a more structured setting, Usability Testing allows researchers to focus on specific aspects of a product’s functionality. Distinctive elements include:

  • Controlled setting: Testing occurs in a lab or a simulated environment;
  • Task-oriented: Participants are given specific tasks to perform;
  • User-interface focus: Emphasis on navigation, layout, and accessibility.

Data Collection Techniques

Usability Testing Data Collection:

  • Quantitative focus: Relies on measurable data like task completion time and error frequency;
  • User feedback: Involves surveys and questionnaires post-task completion;
  • Analytical evaluation: Utilizes performance metrics to assess usability.

5 Guidelines for Conducting a Test

Here are tips for putting together a usability test for your website.

Excerpt from: Stage 5 in the Design Thinking Process: Test
  1. Show, don’t tell: let your users experience the prototypeMake sure to introduce yourself. Never, ever say you are the designer, even if you are. People will be less honest with feedback if they think you are the author and won’t want to hurt your feelings. Explain how long the session should take, what your expectations are for them and what they are going to be doing. Always ask if they have any questions before starting.

    Avoid over-explaining how your prototype works, or how it is supposed to solve your user’s problems. Let the users’ experience in using the prototype speak for itself, and observe their reactions.
  2. Ask Participants to Talk Through Their ExperienceWhen participants are exploring the prototype, ask them to tell you what they’re thinking. Let them know that they should think out loud and speak what’s on their minds during the entire test session. This doesn’t come naturally to people, so you may have to prompt the participant during the test to remind them. In your intro to the test, make sure to let them know you are expecting this and give an example. You want them to let you know what they expect to happen when they select something or what they were expecting to see on the screen based on the title or location.
  3. Observe Your ParticipantsBe a neutral observer. Observe how your participants use your prototype and resist the urge to correct them when they misinterpret how it’s supposed to be used. Mistakes are valuable learning opportunities. Remember that you are testing the prototype, not the participant.
  4. Ask Follow-Up QuestionsAlways follow up with questions, even if you think you know what the participant means. Ask questions such as “What do you mean when you say ___?”, “How did that make you feel?”, “What did you expect would happen?” and, most importantly, “Why?”.
  5. Negative Feedback is Your Way to Learn and ImproveWhen you test your ideas and prototypes, remember that negative feedback is an important way to learn and improve. You might feel a sting in the moment when you hear a person complain about how difficult your prototype is to use, but try to get used to the idea that such feedback will help you in the long run. You will uncover problems that you and your team might not have even considered.
