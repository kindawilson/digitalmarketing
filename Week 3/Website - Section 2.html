Short-Term Memory and Related Strategies

These strategies help reduce cognitive load by chunking content, simplifying choices, emphasizing important items, and using familiar website patterns.

Key Takeaways

  • Organize content into smaller chunks to help users process, understand, and memorize easily. Avoid the wall of text.
  • Simplify the options and highlight the recommended ones
  • Make important information different
  • Use website models that consumers are familiar with

Reducing the Cognitive Load

How can we reduce the cognitive load so users aren't so overwhelmed?

  • Chunking can organize content to help user process, understand, and memorize things more easily. It involves grouping related content and objects together by using design elements such as color, scale, dividers, and spacing (design principle of proximity).
  • Avoid the dreaded "wall of text" - particularly when someone first lands on a site. The longer a post is, the less likely people will be to read it all the way through. Chunk it down and use dividers.
NoteNOTE that this doesn't necessarily apply in every situation - such as content that might be viewed more like prose, such as the origin story of a product, etc.

Hick's Law

Action: Simplify the options, highlight the recommended ones

Hick's Law states that the time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available. This leads to the Paradox of Choice which states that more is NOT always better. More choices often lead to choice overload and can increase the amount of stress we feel when making a complex decision.

When simplifying your content, eliminate only the unnecessary complexity (but don't take it too far). What is the MOST IMPORTANT thing you want people to do? What is the goal of the interaction? Narrow it down and focus on that. Make it easy.

What can we do so that we don't overload users?

  • Highlight the recommended options.
  • Break complex tasks into smaller steps to decrease the cognitive load. Utilize progressive onboarding to minimize the cognitive load for new users (this relates to Tesler's Law).
  • Be careful not to simplify too much to the point of abstraction.

Von Restorff

Action: Make important information different. Chill out on the visual elements, including motion

The Von Restorff effect, also known as the Isolation Effect, predicts that when multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered. How can we create a user experience that is most likely to be remembered?

  • Make important information or key actions visually distinctive.
  • Use restraint when placing emphasis on visual elements to avoid them competing with one another and to ensure salient items don't get mistakenly identified as ads.
  • Don't exclude those with a color vision deficiency or low vision by relying exclusively on color to communicate contrast.
  • Carefully consider users with motion sensitivity when using motion to communicate contrast.

Jakob's Law

Action: Use website mental models that consumers are used to

Users spend most of their time on other sites. This means that users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know.

Users form mental models of what things mean and what they should do. A mental model is what the user believes about the system (web, application, or other kind of product) at hand. Mental models help the user predict how a system will work and, therefore, influence how they interact with an interface. - Nielsen Norman Group

  1. People expect websites to act alike.
  2. Each user has their own mental model
  3. Mental models can change over time
