Section 2

Short-Term Memory and Related Strategies

Key Takeaways

  • Organize content into smaller chunks to help users process, understand, and memorize easily. Avoid the wall of text.
  • Simplify the options and highlight the recommended ones
  • Make important information different
  • Use website models that consumers are familiar with

Knowing what we do now about short-term memory, we can start looking at some of the mental shortcuts that humans make, their specific consumer behaviors, and some design elements we can implement to have more effective digital marketing.

One thing that’s probably very intuitive for you is that we often don’t have the capacity (or motivation) to read every single word on a page and fully weigh out and process every single option in a decision. Have you ever looked at a huge book - or even a huge page full of information - and felt overwhelmed? YEP. Your customers have as well.

Because of this limited working memory, the brain has developed a few shortcuts for deciding which information is important and which should be be paid attention to. We can help the customer by guiding them through the information they need to know and pointing them toward decisions. We can also design our digital information so it doesn’t overwhelm customers.

Our goal is to guide the customer and take away any friction or barriers to purchase, thus helping it make a good experience for them as well.

Read below for some of the most well-known “Laws of UX” - the laws that we have found to be true for consumer behavior and some simple tips to design regarding those. (Summaries below excerpts and adapted from https://www.lawsofux.com )

Miller’s Law - Reducing the Cognitive Load

We know that consumers can hold a limited amount of short-term memory and can get overwhelmed with too much information. How can we reduce the cognitive load so users aren’t so overwhelmed?

Action Items:

  1. Avoid the dreaded “wall of text” – particularly when someone first lands on a site. The longer a post is, the less likely people will be to read it all the way through. Hick’s Law

Hick’s Law states that the time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available. This leads to the Paradox of Choice which states that more is NOT always better. More choices often lead to choice overload and can increase the amount of stress we feel when making a complex decision.

When simplifying your content, eliminate only the unnecessary complexity (but don’t take it too far). What is the MOST IMPORTANT thing you want people to do? What is the goal of the interaction? Narrow it down and focus on that. Make it easy.

Von Restorff’s Law

The Von Restorff effect, also known as the Isolation Effect, predicts that when multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered. How can we create a user experience that is most likely to be remembered?

Action Items:

  1. Make important information or key actions visually distinctive.
  2. Use restraint when placing emphasis on visual elements to avoid them competing with one another and to ensure salient items don’t get mistakenly identified as ads.
  3. Don’t exclude those with a color vision deficiency or low vision by relying exclusively on color to communicate contrast.
  4. Carefully consider users with motion sensitivity when using motion to communicate contrast.

Jakob’s Law

Users spend most of their time on other sites. This means that users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know. People expect websites to look and act alike. Otherwise, they would need to re-learn how to interact with each website individually.

“Users form mental models of what things mean and what they should do. A mental model is what the user believes about the system (web, application, or other kind of product) at hand. Mental models help the user predict how a system will work and, therefore, influence how they interact with an interface.” - Nielsen Norman Group.
